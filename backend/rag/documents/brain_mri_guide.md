# Brain MRI — Clinical Interpretation Guide

**Document Type:** Curated Educational Reference
**Source:** Compiled from standard radiology and neurology textbooks

---

## Normal Brain MRI Anatomy

A normal brain MRI shows symmetric gray and white matter with no focal lesions, mass effect, or midline shift. The ventricles should be symmetric and age-appropriate in size. The cortical sulci and cisterns should appear normal without evidence of volume loss beyond what is expected for the patient's age.

## White Matter Hyperintensities

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) appear as bright areas on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences. They are commonly associated with:

- **Small vessel disease**: Chronic hypertension causes damage to small penetrating arteries, leading to periventricular and deep white matter lesions.
- **Age-related changes**: Mild periventricular caps and thin pencil-like rims are common in older adults and may not indicate significant pathology.
- **Demyelination**: Multiple sclerosis plaques often appear as ovoid periventricular lesions oriented perpendicular to the ventricles (Dawson's fingers).
- **Chronic ischemia**: Confluent white matter lesions may indicate chronic microvascular ischemic disease, especially in patients with vascular risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension.

### Fazekas Scale for White Matter Lesions

The Fazekas scale grades white matter hyperintensities:
- **Grade 0**: No lesions
- **Grade 1**: Punctate foci
- **Grade 2**: Beginning confluence of foci
- **Grade 3**: Large confluent areas

Higher grades are associated with increased risk of cognitive decline, stroke, and dementia.

## Brain Atrophy

Brain atrophy is characterized by widened sulci and enlarged ventricles relative to age-matched norms. Global cortical atrophy (GCA) is rated on a 0–3 scale:
- **GCA 0**: No atrophy
- **GCA 1**: Mild opening of sulci
- **GCA 2**: Moderate volume loss with widened sulci
- **GCA 3**: Severe atrophy with knife-blade appearance

Medial temporal lobe atrophy, particularly involving the hippocampus, is an early marker of Alzheimer's disease and can be assessed using the Scheltens scale (0–4).

## Cerebral Small Vessel Disease

Cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) is a common finding in patients with hypertension, diabetes, and aging. MRI features include:
- White matter hyperintensities
- Lacunar infarcts (small cavitated lesions, typically 3–15 mm)
- Cerebral microbleeds (seen on susceptibility-weighted imaging)
- Enlarged perivascular spaces
- Brain atrophy

SVD is a leading cause of vascular cognitive impairment and contributes to approximately 25% of ischemic strokes.

## Mass Lesions

Mass lesions require urgent evaluation. Key features include:
- **Ring-enhancing lesions**: May indicate abscess, high-grade glioma, or metastasis
- **Diffusion restriction**: Suggests acute infarct or abscess
- **Mass effect**: Compression of adjacent structures, midline shift
- **Edema**: Vasogenic edema (white matter) vs cytotoxic edema (gray and white matter)

## Clinical Significance in Diabetes and Hypertension

Patients with Type 2 Diabetes and Hypertension frequently show accelerated white matter disease. Studies demonstrate that:
- Diabetic patients have 2–3 times higher burden of white matter lesions compared to age-matched controls
- Poor glycemic control (HbA1c > 7%) is associated with faster progression of white matter disease
- Combined hypertension and diabetes synergistically increase the risk of cerebral small vessel disease
- These patients are at higher risk for vascular dementia and cognitive decline
